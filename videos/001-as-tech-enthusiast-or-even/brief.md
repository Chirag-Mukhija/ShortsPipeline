# Brief: Why can't I stick to one thing?
Researched 2026-11-12. YouTube moves fast; recheck before publishing.

## The idea in one line
A self-taught learner explains, through their own chain (networking, custom protocol, Claude Code, backend, system design, AWS/DevOps), why we keep getting pulled sideways, whether that is actually bad, and what to commit to when nothing is guaranteed.

Closest to our topic and small: "Shiny Object Syndrome" videos top out near 5,000 views (kodepatra 24x on 5,010; Web Dev Cody 0.19x of his median). Tutorial-hell videos do better (bigboxSWE 8.4x, 593k; Chai aur Code 6.1x, India). No breakout exists for "shiny object syndrome in tech".

## The gap
1. The shiny-object genre is generic (ADHD, productivity, tutorial hell) and almost never uses a real, dated chain of switches. Yours has named steps a viewer recognizes.
2. The AI-era videos say what to learn (roadmaps, "careers that will explode") and skip the harder question: what do you do when the answer expires? Nobody addresses limited after-college and after-work hours.
3. Few videos use evidence. The deliberate-practice crowd rarely mentions that practice explains a small share of differences in professions [4]. Guru-style "grind" claims are exactly what your tone excludes.
4. Nobody ties switching to a concrete cost or fix: unfinished goals keep nagging until you make a plan [5], and AI can quietly stop you learning [1].

Viewer check: the evidence supports your choice. Learners lean on YouTube more than professionals [3], and early-career developers use AI most [2]. The self-taught / early-career developer is the core; students fit the same mold. Keep your combined default.

## Three angles

**1. "I Switched Tech 5 Times. Was It a Mistake?"**
- (Showrunner: the timeframe isn't in the idea, so it's left out until the creator gives the real one.)
- Promise: an honest audit of your chain, showing what carried over and what was lost, ending with a rule for when to switch.
- Why it can win: personal story is the unfilled slot; Ferriss's 6-minute generalist video hit 23x with a direct question; Lazear found breadth of roles predicts entrepreneurship, but only for founders [7].
- Record/show: an iPad sketch of the chain, your actual project folders, the Claude Code purchase moment.
- Risk: "carried over" is my inference (networking, system design and DevOps share ideas like latency and scaling), not a sourced result. You must confirm it from your own experience.

**2. "Your Shiny Idea Isn't Laziness. Park It Properly"**
- Promise: a simple system: one main project, one capped side-quest slot, and a written parking note for every new idea.
- Why it can win: Masicampo and Baumeister found a specific plan removed an unfinished goal's mental interference [5]. Practice testing and spaced practice are the best-rated study methods [6]. Fear-of-wasted-time hooks perform (DevForge 85x).
- Record/show: your idea list, a screen recording of the parking note, a week-by-week time budget for a student or worker.
- Risk: evidence is lab studies on everyday goals and students, not developers [5][6]. Say that plainly.

**3. "AI Can Code. So What Should I Actually Learn?"**
- Promise: durable skills over tool names, and how to learn with AI so you really learn.
- Why it can win: AI-era outliers (Loh 52x, Piech 32x, Ng 18x) show demand. Anthropic's trial: 50% vs 67% quiz scores when engineers learned a new library with AI, driven by how they used it [1]. 64% of developers now use AI to learn [2].
- Record/show: yourself building with Claude Code, asking for explanations versus delegating, then answering a quiz.
- Risk: the study used a sidebar assistant, 52 people, a short task [1]. Anthropic made it, so name that conflict on camera.

## Proposed takeaway (research-backed, no guarantee)
"Nothing is guaranteed. But you can improve your odds: finish small real things on one main thing, learn by recalling and spacing, ask AI to explain rather than do, and park new ideas instead of chasing them."
Honest limits: no study compares staying with one stack for a year against sampling. Practice explains little of professional differences [4]. Treat "one year on one thing" as your judgment, not a finding.

## Facts the script can use
- Experiment: 52 engineers learning a new library; AI group scored 50% vs 67% by hand, largest gap on debugging; not meaningfully faster [1].
- High scorers asked conceptual questions; delegators scored under 40% [1].
- Limits: sidebar assistant, quiz right after, small sample [1].
- 64% of developers use AI to learn, up from 44% and 37%; only 1% use it alone [2].
- Early-career developers use AI daily most (68%) [2].
- Learners use YouTube more than professionals (70% vs 60%) [3].
- Practice explained 26% of variance in games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, under 1% professions [4].
- Specific plans removed unfinished-goal interference in lab studies [5].
- Practice testing and spaced practice rated high utility; rereading and highlighting low [6].
- Breadth of roles best predicted Stanford alumni entrepreneurship [7].
