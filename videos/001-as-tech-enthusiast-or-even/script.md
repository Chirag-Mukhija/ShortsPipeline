## Beat 4: The real cost isn't switching. It's not learning (~3:30-5:15)

The AI group averaged 50% on the quiz. The hand-coding group, 67%. The biggest gap was on debugging. And the AI group was about two minutes faster, but that gap wasn't statistically significant. [src: 1]

[ON SCREEN: GRAPHIC: two hand-drawn bars fill in, "AI 50%" and "by hand 67%", with a small bracket and the word "debugging" marking the biggest gap. Numbers in the clean sans.]

Here's the part that matters to me. The lowest scorers, under 40%, handed the work to the AI, or leaned on it more and more. The people who asked conceptual questions, or asked for explanations along with the code, averaged 65% or more. [src: 1]

## Beat 6: Learn skills, not tool names (~6:30-7:45)

Here's what I noticed about the chain. In my chain, the tool names kept changing. The thinking underneath didn't. Breaking a problem down, working out why something broke. That's why that debugging gap in the study stuck with me. [own]

[ON SCREEN: GRAPHIC: the chain links split into two piles. "tool names" on the left, each one crossed out with a quick scribble as it goes stale; "skills" on the right, drawn in the accent colour and kept.]

Does practice fix everything? No. A meta-analysis of 88 studies found that practice explained 26% of the differences in performance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, and under 1% in professions. Software engineering wasn't one of the areas they looked at. [src: 4]

The way the study's press release summed it up: practice won't make everyone perfect, but it will make almost everyone better. So practice helps. It isn't magic. [src: 4, press release paraphrase, not the authors' words]

## Beat 7: How to learn with AI so you actually learn (~7:45-9:15)

So here are three rules. They're what I'm trying. They're not guaranteed. [own]

One. Ask AI to explain, not just to do. Same task, two ways. If I ask "do it", I get code. If I ask "explain why, then I'll write it", I get something to type and think about. That's closer to the people who scored high in the study, though remember, it can't prove cause. [src: 1]

[ON SCREEN: SCREEN: a Claude Code session, split in two. Left: the same small task asked as "do it". Right: asked as "explain why, then I'll write it". The creator's real recording; short captions drawn on top: "do it" and "explain first".]

Two. Before you move on, try it again without AI. Close the chat, and write it from memory. That's testing yourself, and doing it again tomorrow and next week is spacing it out. [src: 6] The study behind that used students, so this is my version of it. [own]

[ON SCREEN: GRAPHIC: a hand-drawn calendar strip with three small ticks: "today", "tomorrow", "next week", each labelled "no AI".]

Three. When a shiny new idea shows up, and it will, because the next video is always right there, don't chase it and don't just ignore it. Write a short plan for it, and park it. In lab studies, people who made a specific plan for an unfinished goal found it stopped interfering with what they did next. Those were lab studies, not about programming, so take it as a nudge. [src: 5]

[ON SCREEN: SCREEN or GRAPHIC: the creator's real parking note, one new idea with three lines: "what it is", "first step", "when I'll look at it again". Handwritten style, a paper clip drawn on the corner.]

Then I go back to the main thing. That's it.
